How was this Atlas constructed?

There is no single “one true model” of the data lifecycle. We therefore use NIST RDaF as an authoritative backbone, then decompose it operationally into ten control points so the model can be applied and audited.

Methodological rule: the ten stages below are an operational synthesis, not a new standard. Metadata, provenance, quality, security, ethics, and cost are cross-cutting functions; they do not wait for a single stage.

Six distinctions that prevent common conceptual errors

FAIR ≠ Open

FAIR permits authentication and authorization where needed. Restricted data can still be FAIR when access conditions are described clearly.

FAIR ≠ intrinsic quality

FAIR helps people find, understand, and reuse data, but it does not establish that a measurement is correct or a sample is representative.

Backup ≠ Preservation

Backup primarily supports recovery; preservation adds long-term integrity, context, migration, policies, and accountability.

Integrity ≠ Authenticity

A checksum can show that bits have not changed from a baseline; by itself, it cannot establish that an object is the authoritative copy or that its claimed origin is correct.

Metadata ≠ Provenance

Metadata describes an object; provenance describes how it came to be and who or what participated in producing it. They can overlap, but they are not synonyms.

Reproducibility ≠ Replicability

This Atlas follows NASEM: reproducibility is computational, using the same data, code, and conditions; replication tests for consistent findings through a new study or new data.

Models and standards: what does each one do—and not do?

A serious methodological error is to collapse FAIR, OAIS, PROV, and DCC as though they were versions of the same model.

NIST RDaF 2.0 as the backbone

RDaF describes a cyclical rather than linear lifecycle: Envision → Plan → Generate/Acquire → Process/Analyze → Share/Use/Reuse → Preserve/Discard. Movement between stages is flexible, and themes span multiple stages. This Atlas decomposes those regions into more precise operational control points without claiming a new standard taxonomy.

Why not use FAIR as a lifecycle? FAIR describes principles for digital objects. The original paper states that the principles do not prescribe a specific technology or implementation, and are not themselves a standard or specification.

Important rule: late detection does not mean the error began late

A failure may be detected during reuse even though it originated during generation or processing. Each stage should therefore produce evidence that the next stage can test, rather than handing over “just a file.”

Critical Product Audit

This is not a marketing page. It states the limits of what can be defended and what should not be claimed.

1. There is no single canonical lifecycle.

RDaF is used as the backbone because it is current, comprehensive, developed through broad participation, and explicitly cyclical and tailorable. DCC adds a curation perspective; OAIS focuses on preservation.

2. Stage boundaries are partly artificial.

In practice, metadata, provenance, security, quality, and cost operate throughout the lifecycle. They are separated into boxes here only for operational clarity.

3. These Quality Gates are not standards.

Measures such as metadata completeness and provenance coverage are useful for management, but no universal threshold automatically makes a dataset “good.”

4. Generic RDM is insufficient for materials.

Scientific reuse requires domain metadata. The Atlas therefore adds a materials lens and points to NOMAD and OPTIMADE rather than inventing a general ontology.

5. FAIR does not establish validity.

A biased or incorrect dataset can be made more findable. Measurement, study-design, and analysis validity require independent controls.

6. Preservation is not merely storage infrastructure.

OAIS, NDSA, PREMIS, and CoreTrustSeal require responsibilities, processes, context, and monitoring—not object storage alone.

7. Failure examples do not generalize.

The figures 19.6%, 17% per year, and 1 in 5 are tied to specific study designs and contexts. They are used as documented examples, not as global prevalence estimates.

8. Legal and privacy requirements are context-specific.

The Atlas treats ethics, rights, and security as cross-cutting functions, but does not claim to provide universal legal compliance or replace local review.

9. Terminology can differ between communities.

The Atlas follows NASEM when distinguishing reproducibility from replicability, but other fields may reverse the usage. Every paper or project should therefore state its definitions.

10. The version date matters.

What can be claimed with confidence?

Defensible

  • The data lifecycle is non-linear and includes cross-cutting themes.
  • Provenance, metadata, preservation, persistent identifiers, and rights are distinct and complementary dimensions.
  • Backup alone is not preservation.
  • FAIR is neither quality nor necessarily open.
  • Reuse exposes gaps that may not appear when a file is created.

Should not be claimed

  • The ten stages are a universal standard.
  • One metric measures all of “data quality.”
  • A FAIR dataset is automatically scientifically valid.
  • A trusted repository makes all its contents correct.
  • RO-Crate or a container guarantees reproducibility without testing.
